# Rosie — the seven use cases we design around

Owner's brief, 2026-09-18. Every screen, model and number in Rosie exists to serve one of these. Each use case names the decision it supports, who makes it, what triggers it, the inputs it needs, the workflow, the outputs it must produce, the design requirements that cannot be traded away, and how its value is proved. The gap map against the current build is in `use-cases-gap.md`.

---

## 1. Determine whether a program can expand

**Decision to support.** Can the institution increase enrollment, add a cohort, open a new delivery location, or launch a new program — and still produce the promised number of qualified workers?

**Primary users.** College president, provost or chief academic officer · workforce or continuing-education vice president · dean · program director · CFO or budget officer · regional workforce intermediary · employer or funder evaluating an expansion proposal.

**Triggers.** Employers request more graduates · a labor-market analysis identifies an occupational shortage · the institution receives a grant opportunity · the state considers funding additional seats · a program has more qualified applicants than seats · a college proposes an evening, weekend, accelerated, satellite or dual-enrollment offering · leadership wants to launch a new program · another institution reduces or closes a program · a facility expansion creates potential new capacity.

**Inputs required.** Annual target for qualified, placed or fully productive workers · current enrollment capacity · actual enrollment and historical seat fill · applicant-to-enrollment conversion · completion, licensure, placement, retention and productivity rates · program length and course sequence · classroom, laboratory, simulation and clinical requirements · faculty workload and qualification requirements · preceptor ratios and availability · clinical-site agreements, assets, shifts and capacity · room and equipment availability · accreditation or regulatory limits · institutional calendar and holidays · student travel constraints · expansion costs and available funding · time required to recruit faculty, secure sites, purchase equipment and obtain approvals.

**Core workflow.**
1. Define the desired annual workforce output.
2. Select the year by which the target must be reached.
3. Work backward through the talent pipeline: required productive workers → regional placements → license passers → completers → enrolled students → offers → qualified applicants → interested candidates.
4. Translate required enrollment into dated cohorts.
5. Apply the program's term and course structure.
6. Calculate required faculty instructional hours, peak concurrent faculty, preceptor hours and head count, clinical learner-shifts, clinical settings and experiences, classroom hours, laboratory hours, specialized equipment, student-support capacity.
7. Test every requirement against current supply.
8. Identify the first binding constraint and all secondary constraints.
9. Model alternative expansion designs: larger existing cohort · additional annual cohort · evening cohort · weekend cohort · accelerated schedule · additional campus · shared regional program · hybrid instruction · expanded clinical geography · improved retention instead of additional enrollment.
10. Determine the earliest feasible start date.
11. Calculate the annual output after the expansion reaches steady state.
12. Produce an approval-ready recommendation.

**Required outputs.** Current maximum feasible cohort size · proposed cohort size · additional annual enrollment, completions, licensure passers, regional placements, productive workers · first year additional workers enter the labor market · faculty FTE and people required · preceptor FTE and people required · clinical learner-shifts required · clinical sites and settings required · room and equipment requirements · student-support requirements · binding constraints · cost by category · cost per additional completer · cost per additional placed worker · risk and confidence level · assumptions requiring verification · recommended expansion scenario · implementation milestones and owners.

**Critical design requirements.** Distinguish target, forecast, committed capacity, scheduled capacity and actual results · show both FTE and number of people · never present theoretical capacity as usable capacity · identify when capacity exists overall but not on the required dates, shifts or settings · show time to impact · allow assumptions to change without altering the operating plan · preserve multiple named scenarios · show source, owner, date and verification status of every material assumption · display why a scenario is infeasible, not just that it is · produce an executive summary and a detailed operational trace back to courses, shifts, sites and people.

**Proof of value.** Time to complete an expansion analysis · previously hidden constraints identified · unsupported proposals avoided · additional seats made operationally feasible · additional completions and placements produced · capital or staffing expenditure avoided · cost per additional worker · forecast vs actual · time from employer request to an approved, feasible response.

## 2. Identify the best investment for increasing qualified-worker production

**Decision to support.** Given limited funding, which combination of interventions will produce the greatest credible increase in qualified, regionally placed workers?

**Primary users.** College cabinet · CFO · foundation or grants office · community college system · workforce board · chamber or regional intermediary · employer coalition · philanthropic funder · state agency · legislative or executive-branch decision-maker.

**Triggers.** One-time or recurring funding · an employer offers support · a foundation grant opportunity · state workforce funds · programs competing for the same funding · a choice between facilities, faculty, student support and clinical partnerships · deciding which institution should expand · existing funding not producing expected outcomes.

**Investment options to evaluate.** Full-time faculty position · adjunct faculty · faculty compensation · faculty credential attainment · clinical coordinator · paid or supported preceptors · clinical-site partnership staff · new clinical agreements · expanded site operating shifts · simulation equipment · specialized laboratory equipment · renovated or new instructional space · evening or weekend delivery · transportation to clinical sites · childcare or emergency aid · tutoring or licensure preparation · recruitment · applicant conversion · first-term retention · reduced withdrawal · licensure pass rates · regional placement · shorter completion-to-employment · shared faculty, facilities or sites across institutions.

**Inputs required.** Available funding and whether recurring · intervention costs · implementation lead time · useful life of equipment or facilities · existing binding constraints · expected effect of each intervention and the evidence for it · current conversion rates · current delivery capacity · enrollment demand · accreditation requirements · faculty and preceptor availability · clinical-site capacity · student-support needs · regional placement demand · risk and uncertainty · sustainability after grant funding ends.

**Core workflow.**
1. Define the outcome to maximize. 2. Establish total funding. 3. Identify current binding constraints. 4. Generate eligible interventions. 5. Estimate each one's effect on enrollment capacity, seat fill, completion, licensure, placement, productivity and time to impact. 6. Detect interventions that do not address the binding constraint. 7. Eliminate investments that add unusable capacity. 8. Test combinations, because one investment may unlock another. 9. Calculate recurring and one-time costs. 10. Calculate marginal output. 11. Apply risk and confidence adjustments. 12. Compare scenarios. 13. Recommend the portfolio with the best credible return. 14. Establish milestones and outcome measures.

**Required outputs.** Baseline annual production · expected production per scenario · incremental workers · one-time and recurring cost · cost per additional seat, completer, license passer, regional placement · time to first and full impact · dependencies · risks · sensitivity range · confidence · sustainability risk · recommended portfolio · investments rejected because they do not address the actual constraint.

**Critical design requirements.** More enrollment does not automatically mean more workers · interventions combine · no double-counting across interventions · capacity creation ≠ utilization · seats ≠ outcomes · include lead time · treat evidence quality explicitly · conservative, expected and optimistic estimates · show when retention beats expansion, when a clinical coordinator beats equipment, when a building adds nothing because faculty or clinical capacity binds · funders can monitor whether the funded intervention produced the promised result.

**Proof of value.** Workers per dollar · share of funding directed at verified constraints · low-value investments avoided · time to prepare a recommendation · accuracy of projections · dollars secured · increase in completions or placements · share of funded initiatives meeting targets · investments redesigned after constraint analysis.

## 3. Create a feasible clinical-placement plan

**Decision to support.** How should every required clinical learner-shift be assigned to an eligible site, asset, date, shift and preceptor while satisfying program, accreditation, agreement, travel and student constraints?

**Primary users.** Clinical coordinator · program director · department chair · academic scheduler · clinical-site liaison · faculty member responsible for placements · dean overseeing multiple clinical programs.

**Triggers.** New term schedule · added cohort · enrollment change · new site agreement · loss of a site · reduced site capacity · preceptor departure · student accommodation · accreditation change · holiday or calendar change · site cancellation · faculty absence · conflict detected.

**Inputs required.** Named students or enrollment targets · required hours and experiences · courses and rotation types · required settings · dates and shift lengths · site assets and seats · asset operating days and shifts · agreement status · site-specific restrictions · preceptor availability and qualifications · instructor availability · max students per preceptor and per asset · student home location or travel limit · student availability and accommodations · continuity preferences · variety requirements · health-system and facility-type requirements · college and site holidays · existing manual assignments · locked assignments · accreditation ratios · program-specific placement rules.

**Core workflow.** 1. Convert every requirement into dated learner-shift demand. 2. Determine eligible settings and assets per shift. 3. Remove sites failing agreement, qualification, date, shift or capacity. 4. Apply travel rules. 5. Apply preceptor and instructor requirements. 6. Protect locked and manual assignments. 7. Assign by the selected priorities (secured first · shortest travel · site continuity · balanced partner load · preceptor continuity · variety · reduced disruption · fewer split sections). 8. Identify unplaced shifts. 9. Categorize each failure reason. 10. Recommend the smallest rule or supply change that would place the shift. 11. Compare scenarios. 12. Preview the operational changes. 13. Route for approval. 14. Apply. 15. Monitor cancellations and exceptions.

**Failure reasons to distinguish.** No eligible site offers the setting · eligible assets full · capacity on a different day · capacity on a different shift · physical capacity but agreement not secured · seats but no qualified preceptor · preceptor available but over workload · travel exceeds limit · holiday invalidates the session · continuity rule prevents placement · variety not satisfied · instructor conflict · student conflict · requirement incomplete or unverified · source data missing or stale.

**Required outputs.** Learner-shifts required, placed, unplaced, share placed · learner-hours placed and unplaced · students, programs, courses affected · placement by site, setting, date and shift · named preceptors · unstaffed placed shifts · travel-time distribution · agreement-tier distribution · site-load concentration · unplaced by reason · required additional capacity · recommended site requests · recommended schedule changes · comparison with the applied plan · complete change preview · approval and rollback record.

**Critical design requirements.** Explainable engine · lockable assignments · manual bookings never overwritten silently · scenarios separate from the live calendar · apply shows exactly what is created, moved or unchanged · large changes require approval · every applied plan versioned and reversible · site capacity by date and shift, never an annual total · physical, secured, staffed and booked capacity kept distinct · student privacy in partner-facing schedules · partial planning supported · users can see which site, preceptor, date or rule change resolves the most unplaced shifts.

**Proof of value.** Scheduling hours saved · share placed by deadline · late changes · canceled clinical days · students delayed · average travel time · preceptor utilization · site-load concentration · partner satisfaction · share of placed shifts with named qualified preceptors · time to recover from a cancellation · reduction in spreadsheet and email reconciliation.

## 4. Forecast faculty and preceptor needs early enough to act

**Decision to support.** How many faculty and preceptors are required, with which qualifications, during which weeks and shifts, and where do confirmed assignments fall short?

**Primary users.** Dean · department chair · program director · faculty scheduler · clinical coordinator · HR recruiter · budget officer · provost.

**Triggers.** Budget development · semester scheduling · cohort expansion · new program proposal · resignation or retirement · preceptor loss · enrollment change · curriculum change · new accreditation ratio · increased clinical requirements · adjunct-to-full-time conversion · employer request for more cohorts.

**Inputs required.** Course and session schedule · enrollment target by cohort and term · section capacity · faculty-to-student and preceptor-to-student ratios · contact-hour assumptions · workload policy · release time · administrative assignments · faculty and preceptor qualifications · availability by date and time · employment status · maximum workload · current assignments · site affiliations · planned leave · vacancies · compensation and hiring assumptions.

**Core workflow.** 1. Convert every class, lab and clinical session into staffing demand. 2. Cumulative semester workload. 3. Peak concurrent weekly and daily demand. 4. FTE → minimum number of people. 5. Match named people to eligible work. 6. Unfilled work. 7. Overload and underutilization. 8. Double-booked weeks. 9. Forecast shortages by term and program. 10. Evaluate options: full-time hire · adjunct · overload · schedule change · cohort-size change · section consolidation · shared faculty · employer-provided preceptors · simulation substitution where allowed · delayed start. 11. Hiring and assignment plan. 12. Monitor against actual staffing.

**Required outputs.** Faculty FTE by semester · peak weekly FTE · minimum distinct faculty · preceptor FTE by semester · peak preceptor head count · demand by qualification, program, course, site · named assignments · unfilled assignments · overloaded and double-booked people · earliest shortage date · hiring deadline · budget requirement · staffing scenarios · consequence if unresolved.

**Critical design requirements.** FTE and head count separate · cumulative ≠ simultaneous · named assignments separate from modeled requirements · eligibility includes qualifications · preceptors tied to their employers and sites · workload assumptions versioned and sourced · release time and administrative load represented · exact uncovered sessions shown · hiring recommendations include recruitment lead time · distinguish a budget problem from a labor-supply problem · model the loss of a particular person · forecast flows automatically from cohort and schedule changes.

**Proof of value.** Gaps identified before the semester · less last-minute adjunct hiring · less overload · fewer canceled sections · share assigned by deadline · vacancy-to-hire time · budget accuracy · less workload concentration · share of clinical shifts with qualified preceptors · projected vs actual need.

## 5. Keep committed cohorts deliverable as conditions change

**Decision to support.** When something changes after the institution has committed to a cohort, what is now at risk, who is affected, and what is the best recovery action?

**Primary users.** Program director · clinical coordinator · scheduler · department chair · dean · registrar or academic operations · student-support staff · clinical-site liaison.

**Triggers.** Site cancellation · faculty absence or resignation · preceptor withdrawal · room closure · equipment failure · holiday or weather closure · enrollment change · student withdrawal · student accommodation · course cancellation · accreditation change · agreement expiration · site capacity reduction · calendar or term-date change.

**Inputs required.** Current applied schedule · named students · locked assignments · faculty and preceptor assignments · room and asset bookings · site agreements · progression requirements · prerequisites · completion requirements · remediation deadline · replacement resources · communication and approval responsibilities.

**Core workflow.** 1. Detect or record the change. 2. Identify every directly affected record. 3. Trace downstream effects across students, courses, sections, clinical hours, graduation dates, faculty and preceptor workload, rooms, sites, licensure eligibility, workforce-output commitments. 4. Classify severity. 5. Determine the last safe action date. 6. Generate recovery options. 7. Compare disruption, cost, travel, staffing and timing. 8. Assign an owner. 9. Route approvals. 10. Apply. 11. Notify internal users and produce partner-facing information. 12. Confirm resolution. 13. Preserve the incident history.

**Required outputs.** Exact issue · date detected · data source · students affected · courses and cohorts affected · clinical hours at risk · graduation or licensure consequences · workforce-output consequences · last safe action date · recovery options with cost and disruption · recommended response · owner · approval status · resolution status · change history.

**Critical design requirements.** The operational home page prioritizes unresolved exceptions · alerts are consequential, not noisy · each alert names a decision owner · informational warnings distinct from release-blocking problems · recovery preserves locked assignments · recovery can be simulated before applying · downstream effects shown before commit · communications generated from approved data without exposing student information · exceptions have deadlines, status, ownership and escalation · repeated failure patterns feed planning assumptions.

**Proof of value.** Time to identify and to resolve · students kept on schedule · canceled sessions avoided · clinical hours recovered · less last-minute rescheduling · less partner confusion · share of critical exceptions resolved before the last safe date · graduation delays prevented · planned vs actual delivery.

## 6. Diagnose where the student pipeline is failing

**Decision to support.** Which stage of the student-to-worker pipeline is preventing the program from meeting its goal, for which students, and which intervention is most likely to improve the result?

**Primary users.** Program director · dean · enrollment-management leader · student-success leader · institutional researcher · equity officer · workforce executive · regional intermediary · funder monitoring outcomes.

**Triggers.** Underfilled cohorts · missed enrollment goals · rising withdrawal · declining completion · declining licensure passage · graduates not entering regional employment · equity gaps · missed workforce target · expansion that produced no additional workers · capacity-or-conversion question.

**Pipeline stages.** Interested candidate → qualified applicant → offered admission → enrolled → persisting → completing on time → passing licensure → placed in the target occupation → retained in the region → fully productive under a defined standard.

**Inputs required.** Target and actual at every stage · stage dates · conversion rates · historical benchmarks · application, admission and enrollment records · attendance · grades · clinical progress · withdrawal reasons · licensure attempts and results · employment and placement data · employer verification · regional-retention data · demographic and geographic characteristics · intervention participation · data-quality and matching confidence.

**Core workflow.** 1. Compare target and actual at every stage. 2. Find the stage creating the largest loss in eventual workers. 3. Determine the cause: demand · qualification barriers · admission yield · financial · scheduling · transportation · academic · attendance · clinical placement · licensure · employer hiring · wage or job quality · out-of-region placement · data incompleteness. 4. Compare across cohorts and time. 5. Examine disparities. 6. Leading indicators of withdrawal or failure. 7. Model intervention effects. 8. Conversion vs capacity. 9. Track interventions and results. 10. Reforecast output.

**Required outputs.** Target and actual pipeline · conversion at each transition · number and share lost at each stage · each loss point's contribution to the final gap · cohort comparison · disparities · leading indicators · intervention scenarios · additional workers per intervention · cost per additional completer or placement · confidence and data quality · updated forecast.

**Critical design requirements.** Never label a target as an actual · capacity ≠ enrollment · every stage defined · define placed, retained, fully productive · first-time vs eventual licensure · minimum group sizes for sensitive analysis · restricted access to PII · no unsupported causal claims · association, evidence and confidence shown separately · authoritative records stay in the source system · reconciliation with the SIS · employer verification without exposing education records · show time lag.

**Proof of value.** Seat fill · applicant-to-enrollment conversion · withdrawal · on-time completion · licensure passage · regional placement · equity gaps · workers added without new capacity · earlier identification of at-risk cohorts · forecast accuracy · cost per additional successful outcome.

## 7. Coordinate workforce production across institutions

**Decision to support.** How should multiple institutions, employers and funders divide responsibility and share resources to meet a regional workforce goal?

**Primary users.** Chamber or regional intermediary · community college system · workforce board · state agency · hospital or employer coalition · college presidents · provosts and deans · regional funder · economic-development organization.

**Triggers.** A shortage exceeds one institution's capacity · overlapping expansion proposals · competition for the same clinical sites · employers spanning several service areas · regional or state funding allocation · unused capacity at one institution beside constraint at another · major employer expansion · a program closes · a region wants a production compact.

**Inputs required.** Regional occupational demand · agreed production target · current and forecast production by institution · program capacity · enrollment demand · historical outcomes · faculty supply · clinical-site capacity · shared employer and partner relationships · service areas · student travel patterns · facilities and equipment · program costs · expansion lead times · strategic priorities · data-sharing permissions · funding constraints · employer commitments.

**Core workflow.** 1. Regional target. 2. Occupations, geography, horizon, outcome standard. 3. Aggregate institution production without exposing restricted data. 4. Demand vs current and forecast supply. 5. Per institution: available capacity, binding constraints, time to expansion, outcome performance, cost structure, geographic reach. 6. Shared constraints, especially clinical partners and instructors. 7. Duplicative proposals. 8. Shareable resources. 9. Alternative regional portfolios. 10. Allocate production responsibility. 11. Allocate investment by incremental output and feasibility. 12. Employer and institutional commitments. 13. Monitor. 14. Reallocate when conditions change.

**Regional scenarios.** Expand one high performer · distribute smaller expansions · shared regional cohort · shared specialized faculty · shared lab or simulation facility · coordinated site requests · common preceptor pool · shift a program to the institution with the greatest feasible capacity · transportation to widen clinical geography · improve completion instead of duplicating · employer-supported delivery site · teach-out or continuity plan.

**Required outputs.** Regional target · current and forecast production · gap · institutional contribution · capacity and binding constraints by institution · shared constraints · proposed production and funding allocation · additional output per institution · regional cost per additional worker · clinical-site demand across institutions · potential partner conflicts · shared-resource opportunities · employer and institutional commitments · risks and dependencies · annual milestones · progress.

**Critical design requirements.** Institutions control what is shared · aggregate outcomes without student records · identical definitions across institutions · configured programs, active offerings, target enrollment, actual enrollment, completions and placements kept distinct · comparisons disclose completeness · no ranking on incomparable data · shared site capacity never counted twice · overlapping use of faculty, sites, preceptors and employers modeled · funding recognizes lead time and readiness · institutional ownership preserved · every regional commitment traceable to a feasible institutional operating plan.

**Proof of value.** Additional regional workers · duplicative investment avoided · shared clinical capacity secured · employer commitments · funding to verified constraints · inter-institutional agreements · fewer competing requests to partners · utilization of existing capacity · forecast accuracy · shortage-to-plan time · share of the regional goal backed by feasible institutional plans.
